Color as a Generative Principle in the Work of Sonia Delaunay

Research Summary

01

Introduction and
Research Framework

The history of colour in visual culture cannot be reduced to the problem of filling pre-given forms. According to the fundamental thesis of the research, colour is a structuring and meaning-producing factor capable of creating the visual structure itself. Examining the oeuvre of Sonia Delaunay from this perspective becomes particularly significant because, in her art, colour does not appear as a secondary formal property, but as an autonomous, relational and generative operation. The research interprets this operation within the history of modernism, while at the same time placing it in a theoretical context that extends beyond it: colour does not simply ‘exist’ in the image, but, as a process arising from the relations of colours to one another, organises the image, space, rhythm and the temporality of perception.

The point of departure of the research is the reconfiguration of the historical ontology of colour. With his work Opticks, published in 1704, Isaac Newton placed colour in a new scientific context as an investigable phenomenon of the physical structure of light. The prism experiment demonstrated that white light is not a homogeneous unity, but a system of components of different refrangibility. The prism does not create colours, but separates components already present in light. In this way, the concept of colour became partly detached from the traditional conception of objects as possessing properties and entered scientific thought as a measurable, repeatable natural phenomenon.

The objectifying perspective opened up by Newton, however, did not exhaust the problem of colour. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe’s investigations in Zur Farbenlehre of 1810 brought the subjective and physiological conditions of the experience of colour to the foreground. According to the emphasis of the research document, for Goethe the experience of colour is not merely an external physical phenomenon, but an experience also connected to the physiological and subjective operation of vision. The phenomenon of the afterimage is particularly important: after prolonged exposure to an intense colour, vision generates a complementary colour. The eye, therefore, does not simply register, but participates, through its own operation, in the creation of the experience of colour. Colour consequently becomes a psychophysical event that arises in the interaction of the body, perception and the external world.

A decisive stage in this transformation of thought is constituted by the work of Michel Eugène Chevreul. Through his practical experiences at the Gobelins manufactory, Chevreul observed that the same dyed or colourant-coloured thread appeared different in different environments. The problem therefore lay not in the material, but in the relational system of neighbouring colours. De la loi du contraste simultané des couleurs, published in 1839, was the systematisation of this insight: two colours placed beside one another mutually modify the perception of each other. Neutral grey appears greener beside red, while the red itself appears more intense beside it; in a blue environment, the neutral surface may produce an orange effect, and beside yellow a violet effect. Colour, therefore, does not possess an unchanging visual identity: its concrete appearance derives from the total environmental relation.

This relational concept of colour becomes one of the most important historical preconditions of the Delaunay research. In Neo-Impressionism, Georges Seurat did not merely apply colour theory, but made the laws governing perception part of painterly construction. Through the separate application of pure patches of colour, optical mixing became decisive in place of physical mixing, and the viewer’s perception also contributed to the emergence of a unified visual effect. The research interprets this as ‘visual addition’: the painter does not present a finished colour to the viewer, but creates the conditions under which perception itself realises the unity of colour.

In the work of Sonia Delaunay, however, a further step takes place. The relation described by Chevreul and the optical process activated pictorially by Seurat do not remain explanatory principles of perception, but become autonomous organising principles of image-making. The Orphism of the Delaunay couple no longer uses the interaction of colours to optically reorganise a pre-given objective world, but creates an autonomous visual language in which form derives from colour relations. In the interpretation of Orphism formulated by Guillaume Apollinaire, the internal unity of the painting is ensured not by the recognisability of the object, but by the rhythmic interaction of colours.

The concept of the ‘generative principle’ therefore requires a more precise meaning within this research framework than merely stating that Delaunay painted colourfully. Generative is that operation of colour in which a given colour is not a closed result, but a condition for further visual events: it determines the perceived intensity of its neighbour, modifies the sense of space, directs the movement of the gaze, creates rhythm, and thereby establishes the conditions for the emergence of further relations. In this sense, colour is not decoration, but an operation.

In the present research, I use the concept of the ‘generative’ as my own working definition: it does not mean the technological, algorithmic image generation of contemporary generative art, but such a relational operation of colour as becomes a condition for the emergence of further visual configurations.

The research document defines this operation as a concept of colour that is autonomous, relational, temporal and event-like. Colour cannot be treated merely as a technical parameter or reproducible datum, because its meaning and experiential character arise within a system of relations. This definition gives the interpretation of Delaunay’s oeuvre its genuine theoretical weight: the principle of colour appearing in artistic practice is of interest not as a precursor of some later technology, but because it formulates an alternative model of visual generativity whose centre lies in relation, perception and experience.

Research Development

02

Development of the
Research and Methodology

The initial V1 - V9.1 preprint primarily formulated theoretical theses. On the early horizon of the research, the claim was already present that Delaunay’s colour is not a secondary visual element, but the structuring principle of visual thought. Moving forward, however, did not simply mean listing more works. During the research process, the initial conceptual claim gradually unfolded into several interconnected levels of analysis: historical colour theory, theory of perception, analysis of artistic form, intermedial investigation, structural pattern analysis, and a critical examination of the contemporary concept of generativity.

On the basis of the final research material, the first layer of the methodology is historical-genealogical. The investigation does not treat Delaunay’s art as an isolated modernist phenomenon, but places it in the context of the intellectual and visual legacy of Newton, Goethe, Chevreul and Seurat. The aim is not to establish a simple chain of influence, but to demonstrate how the concept of colour shifted from physical property towards perceptual relation and then towards artistic structuring. The research document interprets this process as an ontological transformation spanning several centuries: colour gradually detaches itself from its original material supports while being reorganised at increasingly high levels of abstraction.

The second methodological layer is relational visual analysis. Its basic premise is that the work is not the sum of elements. A patch of colour, geometric unit or module does not necessarily carry the meaning under investigation in itself; its significance becomes accessible within the network of the entire composition. This becomes particularly clear in the analysis of Plougastel, where the curving colour fields are not organised according to fixed contours. Here, form is not a precondition of the image, but the dynamic result of colour relations. From a methodological perspective, the research therefore rejects an analysis that attempts to reconstruct the work by breaking it down into discrete, mutually independent elements.

The third level is the temporal investigation of perception. In the case of Delaunay’s paintings, the viewer does not ‘see’ the composition in a single moment. The movement of the gaze creates ever new centres of tension, while the interaction of colours continuously modifies the visual balance. The painting thus becomes not merely a spatial structure, but a perceptual event unfolding in time.

The fourth methodological layer is intermedial analysis. A significant advance of the research was the recognition that the generative operation of colour cannot be examined exclusively in painting. Delaunay’s textile design, clothing design and applied arts activity constitute the realisation, on different supports, of the same relational logic. The research therefore does not automatically establish a hierarchy between painting and design, but examines how the structuring capacity of colour changes with changes in the support, the body, movement and use.

Finally, the methodology of the research applies a critical comparison with contemporary digital and algorithmic image culture. At this point, however, it is important to distinguish between analogy and identity. The research does not claim that Delaunay was a ‘digital artist’, nor does it claim that painting is technically identical to algorithmic image-making. The relationship is structural: in both cases, the question may be posed of how a visual configuration arises from the operation of relations rather than from a pre-given, static result.

This methodological development essentially resulted in the transformation of the thesis of the initial preprint — ‘colour is generative’ — into a differentiated research model. In the final material, the physical, perceptual, compositional, spatial, temporal, medial and technological dimensions of colour are now distinguished. One of the strongest methodological results of the research is precisely the connection of these levels without seeking to identify them with one another.

Main Research
Findings and
Detailed Analysis

01

Contrastes simultanés:
when colour becomes
a constitutive factor of form

The generative principle of colour becomes most directly perceptible in Sonia Delaunay’s Contrastes simultanés series of paintings, created in 1912, where colour relations operate as active organising elements of the visual structure. According to the interpretation of the research document, in these works scientific colour theory is no longer merely a theoretical background, but an operation that actually creates the composition. The picture surface is constructed through the juxtaposition of intense colour fields, and unity arises not from some external objective order, but from the continuous tension formed between neighbouring colours.

The red–green, yellow–violet and blue–orange contrasts are not isolated colour pairs. Each colour activates the other. As a consequence of the complementary relation, colour does not close but opens the composition. The viewer’s gaze moves from one field to another, and every new relation generates a new intensity.

This point makes possible a precise art-historical definition of ‘generativity’. Generative operation here is not the presence of some machine algorithm, but an internal compositional dynamic in which one visual state conditions the next. Colour, therefore, is not a property of form, but one of the causes of the emergence of form.

Consequently, the status of geometric forms also changes. Rectangles, curving planes and other geometric units are not autonomous carriers of meaning. Their primary function is to carry colour relations. Thus, to use the terminology of the research, form ‘follows’ colour: its significance derives from the chromatic relation in which it participates. Nor is the contour a traditional dividing line any longer; the meeting of colour fields becomes an optical transition.

The structure of the image is therefore not an assemblage of discrete forms, but a system of ‘interpenetrating chromatic energies’. This is of decisive significance because modernist abstraction must not be understood merely as the abandonment of object representation. In Delaunay’s case, abstraction is a positive construction: the place of objective form is occupied by the relational operation of colour.

02

Bal Bullier:
colour, movement
and temporal structure

The 1913 Bal Bullier appears in the research as the next stage in the development of the generative principle of colour. Here, colour no longer merely creates the internal vibration of a picture surface, but structures an entire sensory environment. The dance hall, music, bodies and the movement of the modern metropolis are not merely represented objects, but experiential layers that become condensed in chromatic dynamism.

The curved and intersecting structures of the red, orange, yellow, blue and green colour fields do not allow the gaze to arrive at a point of rest. Movement does not derive from some represented gesture, but from the directing force of colours. The composition operates as a visual field in which the eye continuously forms new pathways.

Bal Bullier is particularly important because of the temporal aspect. Traditional painting often fixes a single selected moment. Delaunay, by contrast, condenses the temporal extension of movement within a single visual presence. Rhythm does not consist of successive scenes, but of a network of simultaneous relations. Every displacement of the viewer’s gaze activates a new rhythmic relation.

The temporal structure of the painting is therefore not narrative time. It is rather perceptual time: the duration during which visual relations gradually become accessible. In this model, colour not only organises space, but also time.

03

Prismes électriques:
the autonomy of
abstraction and light

The 1914 Prismes électriques carries this problem further in the direction of abstraction. The work addresses the modern experience of electric light, to which the observation of the electric illumination of the Boulevard Saint-Michel in Paris was also connected. The painting does not seek a documentary representation of the urban environment, but transforms the visual effects of light and colour into an autonomous visual structure. Electric light here becomes a perceptual event: it contributes to the dissolution of the contours of objects, the loosening of traditional spatial depth, and the perception of the picture surface as chromatic energy.

In this context, the system of concentric circles and circular bands is not merely geometric ornamentation. The circle is a structural model capable of transmitting chromatic relations in every direction. The gaze does not move linearly across the image, but performs expanding and returning movements.

Yet the balance of forces between centre and periphery never becomes final. At one moment the gaze is drawn towards the centre, and at another it turns towards the outward-radiating colour fields. The work thus creates not a stable equilibrium, but constant reorganisation.

At the same time, the repetition of arcs, sectors and segments provides structural discipline. Geometry here does not restrict the movement of colour, but enables its continuous unfolding. Every recurring unit becomes a condition for the emergence of new chromatic relations. This is one of the most important research findings concerning Prismes électriques: geometry and colour are not opposing systems, but two aspects of the same generative process.

04

Textile as a
dynamic chromatic space

Delaunay’s activity as a textile designer carries out a fundamental correction in relation to painting-centred art-historical interpretation. According to the research, textile is not a mere support for painterly results, but an autonomous medium in which the relational logic of simultaneism acquires new dimensions.

In Delaunay’s practice of textile and clothing design, colour leaves the exclusive framework of the flat, static picture surface. The garment is connected to a moving body. Cut lines, seams, folds and pleats are not merely technical necessities, but active factors in the creation of colour relations. When the body moves, the points of encounter between neighbouring colour surfaces change, and new visual balances thereby arise.

The principle of tissu simultané thus transforms colour into a three-dimensional and temporal experience. The wearer is not a mere carrier of the composition, but a participant in its continuous coming into being. Colour is not a finished pattern that the body ‘wears’; the movement of the body actualises the chromatic structure.

The concept of pattern likewise undergoes transformation. Diamonds, zigzags, bands and planar forms become recurring modules that have no isolated significance. Variations in their size, direction and colour relations create new configurations. Repetition, therefore, is not mechanical copying, but rhythmic variation. Textile becomes a structure that can, in principle, be extended infinitely without losing its coherence.

At this point, the concept of generativity already clearly separates itself from simple decorativeness. Decoration adds a motif to a finished structure. In Delaunay’s textile art, by contrast, the structure itself arises from the interaction of colour, geometry, repetition and material.

05

The textile matrix
and structural thinking

The research’s examination of textiles also recalls, as historical antecedents, Chevreul’s experiences with Gobelins weaving and the technological logic of the Jacquard loom. At the Gobelins manufactory, the systematisation of dyed threads made it necessary for colours to be identifiable not as isolated samples, but as an interconnected system. The lightness, saturation and tone of colours became interpretable within a structured space.
